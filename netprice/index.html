What will college actually cost your family?

"We can't predict whether a student will get into a college. We can predict what it will cost if they do."

Every college has a free tool on its website that tells you. This page shows you how to use it, with a real example.

1Every college has two prices

The one on the website, and the one your family would actually pay.

Sticker price

$93,786

Brandeis University, one year, 2026-27, living on campus

The full published cost of attendance for one year: tuition and fees, housing and food, books, transportation, and personal expenses. Almost nobody pays this. At Brandeis, 72% of first-year students received grant aid in 2024-25.

Net price

$6,541

The estimate for our example family (below)

The sticker price minus grants and scholarships, which is money you never pay back. This is the number to compare between colleges. The U.S. Department of Education defines it as "the amount that a student pays to attend an institution in a single academic year AFTER subtracting scholarships and grants the student receives."

Two rules. Never rule a college out because of its sticker price. And never rule a college in because a calculator subtracted loans to make the last number look small. Loans are borrowed money.

A net price calculator is the tool that turns the first number into the second one. Federal law says every college has to post one on its website. You answer questions about your household and income, and the college estimates the grants it would give you. Some take five minutes, some take fifteen, and the answers are different at every college, which is exactly why it is worth doing for every college on your list.

That is the college's estimate of what this family can afford from income and savings. It is a real number; the aid formula treats a $40,000 income for three people as having nothing to spare.

What each block means

  • Cost of attendance is the sticker price for one year. Colleges have to include living costs and books, not just tuition.
  • Grant / gift aid is the money you never pay back. The Pell Grant comes from the federal government and depends only on income and family size; $7,395 is the maximum. The Brandeis grant comes from the college. Brandeis promises full tuition to families with income of $75,000 or less and typical assets, and covers 100% of demonstrated need for every admitted student in year one.
  • Net price is cost of attendance minus grants. This is the number. It is what a family and student would need to cover, one way or another, for the year.
  • Self-help is how the college suggests covering the net price: a federal student loan (borrowed, repaid after college) and a campus job (earned during the year, roughly 8 to 10 hours a week).
  • Remaining cost is what is left after the loan and the job. $41 looks wonderful, and it is genuinely a great result, but it is not the price. The price is $6,541. The loan and the job are how this family would pay it.
The trap to avoid. Some calculators subtract loans first and put the small number in big type, calling it "remaining cost" or "out of pocket." Always compare colleges by net price (after grants only). A college that gets you to $0 with $20,000 in loans is not cheaper than one that gets you to $6,000 with a $3,500 loan.

7Three reactions, and what to do with each

When we run a calculator with a family, the number at the end usually gets one of three reactions. All three are useful.

"OK, we can do that."

Keep the college on the list. Write down the four numbers. When the real aid offer arrives in the spring, compare it to this estimate line by line.

"That's less than we thought."

This happens a lot at colleges that meet 100% of need, and it is the whole reason to run the calculator before crossing a college off. Look at the school seriously.

"That's more than we thought."

Also good to know now, not in April. Run the calculator at two or three other colleges and compare. Ask your college advisor about opportunity programs (HEOP, EOP, SEEK), which are not in any calculator and can lower the price further.

Whatever the reaction, the estimate gives your family a real number to talk about instead of a sticker price or an average. That conversation, at the kitchen table in October, is the point.

What EOP does to a SUNY price

The Educational Opportunity Program, EOP, is the lever most families have never heard of. It is a SUNY program for New York students whose family income is under the state guideline (about $61,000 for a household of four) and whose grades are solid but not automatic admits. It comes with advising, tutoring, a summer start, and its own grant money. Here is a real EOP package a recent SLJ graduate received from SUNY New Paltz for 2024-25, living on campus.

Without EOP, the same family's grants would have been $6,400 smaller and the bill after grants about $10,000. Counted the way the federal table above counts, this package is a net price of about $8,200 a year, against a college average of $10,541 to $13,969 for these income bands.

The takeaway for SLJ families. A residential SUNY, four years away from home, can cost a low-income family nothing up front thanks to EOP, TAP and Pell together. EOP packages across SUNY campuses look roughly like this one. Two rules: check the EOP box on the SUNY application, and know that EOP applicants apply by the regular deadline, not Early Action. Your college advisor screens every student for EOP, HEOP and SEEK eligibility from the financial documents families share in the fall.

What the table is saying

  • A high sticker price can hide a low real price. For a family earning under $48,000, Brandeis (sticker $86,642) cost about the same as Binghamton (sticker $30,657). That is what "meets 100% of need" does.
  • SUNY with EOP is the quiet bargain. New Paltz's average net price for these bands is $10,500 to $14,000; the EOP package above brought it to about $8,200, with nothing due up front. Every residential SUNY has EOP.
  • A popular out-of-state public can be the most expensive option on the list. Penn State's sticker is $24,000 below Brandeis's, and its real price for lower-income families is $5,000 to $7,000 higher. Public universities outside New York rarely give large grants to out-of-state students.
  • CUNY is the floor. For New York City residents, TAP and Pell cover CUNY tuition at most incomes. Every SLJ senior applies to CUNY for exactly this reason.
  • Your estimate can beat the average. Our example family's Brandeis estimate ($6,541) was well below the $15,135 average for its income band. Averages mix in families with savings, a home, or two incomes. The calculator is about you.

9What the calculator can't tell you

It is a very good estimate, not a promise. Researchers who study these tools point to the same handful of limits.

  • It is an estimate for the first year. The real number arrives in the spring, in the financial aid offer, after the FAFSA and any college forms are in. The result page says so at the top.
  • The numbers can be a year behind. Colleges update their calculators on their own schedule. Brandeis's calculator used a sticker price about $3,000 below the university's own 2026-27 bulletin, and its "next steps" text still mentioned the 2024 FAFSA. Treat every number as approximate.
  • Merit scholarships are usually left out. Most calculators estimate need-based grants only. Brandeis says its calculator does not include merit awards. Your offer can be better than the estimate.
  • Opportunity programs are left out too. HEOP at New York private colleges, EOP at SUNY, and SEEK or College Discovery at CUNY are not in any calculator, and they can be worth thousands of dollars a year on top of what the calculator shows (the real EOP package in section 8 added $6,400). Ask your college advisor whether you qualify.
  • Special situations need a phone call. A parent who lost a job this year, large medical bills, a family business, or income from outside the U.S. can all make the estimate wrong. Every college has a financial aid office that will talk to you before you apply.
  • Housing choice changes everything. Living at home instead of on campus lowers the sticker price by about $20,000 at most colleges, and often lowers the grant too. Run it both ways if you are deciding.
  • Results are only as good as the inputs. Household size and total income are the two answers to get right. If the result looks strange, check those two first.

10What happens next

The estimate becomes a real offer through the financial aid applications, and SLJ families do those together with a college advisor.

FAFSA, the federal aid application, opens October 1. SLJ's target is to have every family's submitted by November 26. It takes about an hour when the tax documents are on hand, and it is what unlocks the Pell Grant, New York's TAP grant, federal loans and work-study, and most college grants.

CSS Profile is an additional form that many private colleges, including Brandeis, use for their own grants. Advisors sit with families for this one too, usually in December.

If you would like help with these applications (including FAFSA, TAP, CSS Profile, and HEOP applications), share your 2025 financial documents with the college office: tax returns, or benefit statements for families who do not file taxes, plus W-2s or 1099s. We use them to screen for opportunity programs and to prepare accurate applications, and for nothing else.

And bring your four-number cards. Comparing them with an advisor is the fastest way to build a college list that fits academically, socially, and financially.

11Glossary

Cost of attendance
The college's full estimated cost for one year: tuition and fees, housing and food, books, transportation, personal expenses. Also called the sticker price.
Net price
Cost of attendance minus grants and scholarships. What a family would need to cover for the year.
Grant / scholarship
Gift aid. Money for college that is never repaid. Grants are usually based on need; scholarships are often based on grades, talent, or both.
Pell Grant
The main federal grant, based on income and household size. Up to $7,395 a year. Comes through the FAFSA.
TAP
New York State's Tuition Assistance Program grant, for New York residents at New York colleges. Comes through a short state form after the FAFSA.
Federal student loan
Money borrowed in the student's name and repaid after college. First-year students can borrow up to $5,500; colleges often package $3,500 of it.
Work-study
A part-time campus job, paid with federal money, offered as part of an aid package. It is earned during the year, not taken off the bill in advance.
Demonstrated need
Cost of attendance minus what the aid formula says a family can contribute. The gap the college is being asked to fill.
Meets 100% of need
A college's promise to fill that whole gap with aid, usually mostly grants. Around 90 colleges in the country make this promise; many are highly selective.
Student Aid Index (SAI)
The number the FAFSA produces to measure what a family can contribute. Replaced the old "EFC" in 2024. It can be as low as negative $1,500.
FAFSA
Free Application for Federal Student Aid, at studentaid.gov. Opens October 1 for the following school year. The student and one parent each need their own account.
CSS Profile
The College Board's aid form, used by many private colleges for their own grants, in addition to the FAFSA.
Opportunity programs
HEOP (New York private colleges), EOP (SUNY), SEEK and College Discovery (CUNY): extra aid plus support for students who qualify by income and academic history.
Self-help
The loan and work-study portion of an aid package. The part the student and family provide, as opposed to gift aid.
